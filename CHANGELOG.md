# Changelog

## 1.32.5: Turning sharing on no longer changes what this PC did

- **A lone sharing PC read one number with sharing off and another with it on.** Sharing adds PCs to a table; it does not change what this PC spent, so the two have to agree. The sheet with sharing off already showed the tighter of two bounds, and the table ignored it and worked from one of them. The row is now that same figure, whichever way the switch is set.
- **Two bounds, tight in opposite conditions, and the smaller one wins.** The week minus what was witnessed (the account's percentage moving while this PC spent nothing) is tight on an account where this PC is rarely alone, because idle stretches are then plentiful. This PC's spend times the lowest percent-per-dollar any interval showed is tight where it often is alone, because an interval close to alone then exists. An account of five people and an account of one are opposite cases, and taking the smaller covers both.
- **The lowest ratio is the one nearest the truth, and that is arithmetic rather than a guess.** Between two readings the account's percentage rose for everyone while only this PC's spend is known here, so percentage-over-spend is never below the real figure and equals it when nobody else was working. Other people can only push it up.
- **It is still an upper bound.** Intervals where this PC and somebody else were both working cannot be divided between them, and nothing at all is recorded while the PC is off. The number is at most this, and it tightens on its own as more PCs on the account start sharing: a table of two or more goes back to the exact division, which needs no bound and no estimate.

## 1.32.4: What one PC saw of a week it did not spend

- **1.32.3 took what somebody else spent off the account before dividing the rest, and on a table of two PCs that was the other PC.** The measurement is "the account's percentage moved while THIS PC spent nothing", which is exactly everybody else when one PC shares and includes a teammate who was simply working while you were not when two do. Subtracting it there removes a PC that IS in the table and draws it as one that is not, which is the phantom row 1.30.0 was reported for. It applies to a table of one now, which is the case it was measured for and the case that was broken.
- **A lone sharing PC reads what it can prove rather than the whole account.** Its row is the week minus the part of it that moved while that PC was spending nothing: 55 points witnessed against an account at 75 leaves 20, not 75. No rate is involved, so nothing differs between screens, and the sheet says the row is an upper bound because nothing is recorded while the PC is off.
- **The percentage one dollar buys is not a constant that could have been used instead.** On three accounts whose own rate can be read cleanly, each account's week over that whole account's spend, it came out 0.0319, 0.0364 and 0.0462 percent per dollar: a spread of 1.45x. Fixing a constant from that and multiplying each PC's spend by it would have drawn 8.7% as people who do not exist on the very account 1.30.0 was reported from. The arithmetic here has no rate in it at all.
- **A table of two or more is unchanged.** Those rows are the account's percentage divided by what each PC spent, as they have been since 1.30.0, and that is exact when the PCs in the table are everyone. Telling a teammate's spend from an outsider's needs every PC's hourly history rather than this one's, which the sharing server already stores and this does not yet read.

## 1.32.3: A sharing PC was given the whole account to carry

- **One PC sharing, other people on the account not running this, and the row with that PC's name on it read the entire account's week.** 1.4B tokens and $474 of API-equivalent use drawn as 75% of an Ultra week, where the same work on an account whose PCs all share measures about 15%. A row is the account's weekly percentage times this PC's spend over the table's spend, and with one PC in the table that fraction is one, so the division did nothing and handed over everything, other people's use included. Turning sharing ON made it worse than leaving it off, which at least drew the rest as `Other PCs`.
- **The divisor is the table's spend where it should be everyone's.** Everyone's cannot be read from here, and it is no longer guessed at either. What CAN be read is already on the PC: between two readings of the account's weekly percentage, the percentage rose by some amount and this PC's own spend rose by some amount, and an interval where the second is zero and the first is not is an interval this PC sat out while the week moved anyway. Every point of it belongs to somebody else. Those points are taken off the top before the rest is divided, and they are drawn as their own piece and named.
- **No rate is involved, which is the point.** Percent-per-$ is not a constant, it differs on every PC that measures it, and a remainder computed from it is the bug 1.30.0 was reported for: the same week read 27% on one screen and 19% on another. This is addition over readings both PCs hold, so it comes out the same wherever it is computed. Two PCs that measured wildly different rates draw identical rows, and there is a test that says so.
- **It is a floor and the sheet now says it is.** Nothing is recorded while the PC is asleep, so anything spent by others in that time stays invisible, and the number can only be too small. That makes the rows above it an upper bound rather than a measurement, which is the honest shape of what one PC can know about an account it shares. An account whose PCs all share sees a floor of zero and a screen identical to before.
- The explanation of what the rows include was moved off the sheet into the README in 1.30.0, on the grounds that it was a lot of small print. The one person who needed it could not see it.

## 1.31.0: A market that moves while you are looking at it

- **The price moved once a minute and the chart redrew once a second, which is the wrong way round.** On a one-hour candle chart, a minute of the clock slid all 24 bodies one place along while the newest close, the only figure that is news, moved once an hour. The picture was busy and the number was still.
- **The lattice's step is now a 64th of a minute instead of a minute.** The printed price of a coin changes on 94% to 96% of the seconds it exists for, measured over 20,000 consecutive seconds a coin, against 1.7% before.
- **Not one stored price moved.** 64 is a power of two, so the new grid is an exact subdivision of the old one: every minute boundary is still a block edge, the per-step variance falls by exactly 64 while the lattice grows by exactly 6 levels, and the two cancel to the last bit. 160,712 prices were compared against a table taken off 1.30.1 before the engine was touched, and none of them differ. That is the whole reason it is a 64th and not a second: a second is not a power of two, the minute boundary stops being a block edge, and every `entryTicks` already in storage would price a position at a ratio nobody bought at.
- **A single step of the price still cannot clear the fee.** The widest of 200,000 consecutive steps a coin is 0.0243% on Tidether and 0.0601% on Crabidia against a round trip of 0.5%, so the share of steps whose own move pays for a trade is 0.000% on all four coins. A finer grid is more chances at a smaller number, and both figures are asserted rather than reasoned about.
- **The candles sit on a grid of their own step and only the newest one is part way through.** It spans the grid step to now, so it is short, and its close is the price the extension will trade at. The ones behind it no longer move at all, which also fixes the change figure: the base it is read against moves once a step instead of once a minute, so the percentage stopped drifting while the price stood still.
- **One-minute candles have wicks now.** A minute is 64 steps, so it is sampled like any other candle; the high and low of a one-minute candle used to be its open and its close.
- **The panel asks the host for prices every second instead of once a minute.** One reply costs 0.21ms to 0.31ms depending on the window, which is 13ms to 19ms of a minute's CPU. A refusal and the verdict of a trade now stay on screen until something else is pressed, because a message cleared by the next quiet redraw is a message nobody can read.
- **Prices at the coin's own depth are printed to the tick.** `299.49` rather than `287`: a tick is a hundredth of a credit, and rounding to whole credits threw away the digits that move. Rounded, the printed price changes on 5.3% to 8.4% of seconds; to the tick, on 94.3% to 96.2%. ## 1.30.0: One account's week, the same figures on every PC

- **The same PC's week read 27% on one screen and 19% on another.** One Claude account, one weekly window, two PCs that share it, and the table of PCs disagreed with itself depending on which of them you were looking at. The figure a person quoted to the other person was not the figure the other person could see.
- **The cause was that the split was computed from the percent-per-$ of whichever PC was doing the looking.** Each row was that PC's rate times that row's spend, capped so the rows could not pass the account's weekly %. A PC whose estimate landed under the cap drew small rows; a PC whose estimate hit the cap drew the full account total. Both were drawing the same two PCs' identical reports.
- **A row is now the account's weekly % divided by what that PC spent, at API-equivalent prices.** That needs no rate, it adds up to the account total exactly, and it comes out the same on every PC on the account. The PCs in the reported case read 27.1% and 13.9% from either screen.
- **The row named `Other PCs` is gone.** On an account with two PCs, both of them sharing and both of them in the table, the screen was adding a third row at 13% and calling it other PCs. There were none. That figure was what one PC's rate estimate had left of the account total, and the rows now divide the whole total, so what nobody there reports (claude.ai on the web, the apps, a PC with sharing off) is inside them. The sheet says so in a line under the table instead of drawing a machine that does not exist.
- **`Measured on this PC: about $27 per 1%` no longer says measured.** Percent-per-$ is not a constant to be pinned down: checked against a week of an account's hourly snapshots, the rate over individual intervals ran from 0.027 to 0.083 percent per dollar, threefold, because the weekly limit does not come off in proportion to an API-price equivalent. It reads as an estimate, with the swing named, and where a team split is on screen no rate is quoted at all, because none goes into it.
- The rate is still how a PC that has no table, or has not yet read the account's weekly %, turns its own spend into a percentage. What changed is that it no longer decides how the account's % is divided between PCs, and the reason is written where it is estimated: between two readings the account's % moves for everything on the account while only this PC's own spend is known here, so the ratio is this PC's rate plus whatever the others were doing.

## 1.29.0: The second account's own folder

- **Claude Code's configuration directory is resolved, not assumed.** It was `~/.claude` in one line of `extension.js` and nothing could move it. It is now, in order: the new `claudeUsage.configDir` setting, `CLAUDE_CONFIG_DIR` as the Claude Code extension holds it in `claudeCode.environmentVariables`, `CLAUDE_CONFIG_DIR` in the environment, and then `~/.claude`.
- **The account and the usage came out of two different folders, and that is the part that was not only cosmetic.** The account id was already read from the moved directory, while the tokens were read from the default one. On a PC with weekly sharing on, that meant the right account's group was being sent the other account's figures: a personal account's tokens arriving in a work account's table, under this PC's name. One resolver now answers for both, and nothing else in the extension works out where the directory is.
- **`CLAUDE_CONFIG_DIR` set in a VS Code profile is found.** That variable reaches the Claude Code process and never the extension host, so `process.env` is the one place a profile's choice does not appear. The profile's setting is read directly, and it is read defensively: it belongs to another extension, so any shape it is not expected in simply yields nothing and the resolution carries on.
- **A path that is not there falls back, and says why.** A relative path, a file, or a directory that does not exist is passed over for the next source with the reason written to the Claude Usage output, because falling back in silence looks exactly like the setting being ignored. A leading `~` is the home directory.
- **The directory can move while the window is open.** Changing either setting re-points the readers, re-arms the file watchers on the new directory, and refreshes the panel. The watchers are the part that could not be skipped: `fs.watch` holds the directory it was handed, so a window still watching the old one would show nothing new until it was reloaded.
- **A directory the credit ledger has not counted before is counted from the start.** The ledger's "the past has been counted" mark was about whichever directory counted it, so carried across to a second account it would have skipped that account's history for good. Days merge by taking the larger of two figures, so counting a directory again can only raise a day and can never take credits off the balance. The purse itself (the tables, the market, what the forge paid) is yours, not an account's, and does not move.
- **Sharing is agreed to per Claude account, not per PC.** What the question asks about is who will see this PC's row, and that is the account's other PCs, so switching account changes the people it was answered about. An account that has not answered is off and is asked once, through the same question as before. The answer you had already given becomes the account you are signed in to now, so nobody who had sharing on loses it, and a second account stays off until you say so there too.
- Reported by [aljazk](https://github.com/gnldnd11/claude-usage-monitor/issues/2) in issue #2, who read the source, named the line, and worked out which of the three sources a VS Code profile does and does not reach.

## 1.19.0: A coin market, dice, and ten more games
