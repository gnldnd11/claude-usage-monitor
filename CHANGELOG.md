# Changelog

## 1.44.0: Eight themes become one, and the one is drawn leaf by leaf

- **The shelf sells one theme now, and it was redrawn from nothing.** Seven came off the price list; Forest is the one that stayed. What it was before was a green wallpaper with diagonal stripes and a few ellipses at the edge, because a CSS gradient paints a field of colour and cannot paint a thing. What it is now is 819 leaf blades in seven inline SVG pieces, each with its own angle, its own green and its own place in six layers of depth, baked into coordinates rather than rotated at runtime.
- **The border is foliage and the floor is undergrowth, and they are not mirrors of each other.** Leaves hang down from the top at 35px, stalks run thin down the sides, and grass grows up from a 9px band of soil at the foot. Four things keep the two ends from reading as a decorative frame: the direction of growth, the shape of the blade, two soil colours the canopy never uses, and density waves of 128px and 160px that cannot thicken in the same place.
- **The gauge is made of grass.** The ring reports session use and the only way to read it is where the arc stops, so its shape and its length are untouchable; what changed is the surface inside it. The shell now carries one pattern of blades in the ring's own defs, any theme may point a stroke at it, and the arc keeps every pixel of its path and its end angle.
- **The board behind the stats stopped being a board.** It had no fill at all before, only a 0.56px rounded rectangle, and that line was the whole of why it read as a slab dropped onto the scene. The line is gone, a face in eight gradient layers took its place, light crosses it at the same angle as the scene's own, and a contact shadow underneath is what puts it in the picture rather than on top of it.
- **A gradient this dark arrives as stairs unless it is dithered.** Sampling a rendered row found the green channel stepping 27-26-24-23-20-19-18 with each tread 8 to 12px wide: a 300px ramp near #0f1a10 crosses only a handful of 8-bit values. A 671-byte tile of zero-mean noise now scatters every tread edge, and the longest flat run went from 71px to 6.
- **Nothing in the scene is sized in per cent any more.** A view in a sidebar is whatever height it is dragged to, and a scene tuned at 425px died in the middle at 900. Every layer is pinned in px to the top or the bottom with a band that repeats between them, so the picture is the same at 200px and at 900.
- **The card fills the view while a theme is worn.** The scene, the floor and the motes are children of the card, so they used to stop where it stopped, and a reader who hides the ring and the tiles was left with a short painted card and a tall bare strip under it.
- **A retired theme stays bought.** Six themes keep their directories and their owners keep wearing them; what went is the price and the cell. There is no refund because nothing was taken: the shop's ledger only ever moves one way, and a migration that handed credits back would have to be written once and run once across two windows that share one record.
- **A theme may now set its own frame mask, if it says why.** Forest needs a wider window than the shell's: its corner pieces are 72x40 where the old window was 52x30. Widening the shared one broke the six retired frames, with pieces floating clear of the card, so the shell keeps the window it had and the one theme that needs another opens it in its own block.

## 1.43.0: The panel can be dressed, and the mine rests where it used to freeze

- **Seven panel themes go on the shelf beside the crab drawings.** Coal Face, Forest, Deep Field, Trading Floor, Phosphor, Ocean and Cyberpunk, 10,000 credits each, with the Default free and already yours. A theme owns the surface behind the panel and four layers around it: a frame that leans in from the top corners, a floor the stats stand on, one thing the mascot wears, and a drift of motes.
- **Nothing a theme can reach is a word, a number or a position,** so the panel reads the same in all eight. What a theme may write is checked when it loads: its rules have to live under the usage tab with its own id, and a rule that would reach a game panel is refused rather than shipped. The blackjack felt is outside this feature's blast radius by construction, not by care.
- **A theme is a directory.** `themes/registry.js` finds them, so no shared file names one and adding the eighth is a folder with a `meta.js` in it. The same property the games have had since 1.25.0.
- **A theme's price is counted, not written.** `meta.js` may not carry a price at all: the registry counts the pictures a theme has to draw and reads the rung off the same ladder the mascots use. It counts layers rather than files on purpose, so a theme redrawn as finer artwork later cannot raise the price under someone who already paid.
- **The money follows the shop's existing rules whole.** `theme:` is a second prefix and not a migration, so no mascot key moved and owned and spent stay one record. An unknown `theme:` key now survives a load clean, so a theme dropped from a future build cannot take a purchase with it and leave the money gone.
- **Every theme was judged by rendering it, not by its constants.** Contrast is measured off the shot's own pixels against a 4.5:1 floor, which caught six failures that the token arithmetic passed, one of them a stats row at 1.39:1 under a candle chart. Anything that would cross the text was cut rather than clipped.
- **The mine's resting picture was a 12.6px gap and nothing else.** A swing running at 1,760ms stops between one frame and the next, and from there nothing on the band moves at all, which reads as a crash rather than a rest. A drop now beads at the head and falls, once, as the work stops, and it is gone in 760ms because a thing still falling a minute later says nothing about when. Then a bubble comes up and stays for as long as the window is quiet, with three dots taking turns in it. While the band rests, the count of things moving on it is one.
- **The tool goes down with it, over 160ms.** The drawing, the band, the angle and the glow are untouched, so there is no second state of the pickaxe in the stylesheet to disagree with the first.
- **The claw comes up for a beat when a step is bought.** This screen is open because a reply is slow, so the band a reader is looking at while they press Upgrade is nearly always the resting one, and with the tool down a purchase moved the figure on the button and nothing else: rendered at band 4, step 440, the picture was identical to bare hands at step 1. It fires on the step rather than on the band, because four drawings over five hundred steps means the band would show 4 purchases and leave 496 buying a still screen.
- **Less motion keeps the state and loses the movement.** The gap, the tool going down, the bubble standing up and the beat you paid for all stay; the drop and the breathing do not.
- 15 suites and 57 webview modes pass. The resting moment was read at eight stops between 60ms and 1,000ms and the lift at five, at full resolution, in both themes. Seventeen mutations were run against the mine's tests, and the one that mattered of those it missed, a bubble left standing while the crab is working, is now fenced.

## 1.42.0: A find is worth digging for, and the market gets its own door

- **A find pays sixteen times what it did.** Quartz 1 to 7, garnet 2 to 21, amethyst 3 to 49, the old coin 7 to 140, the crab idol 26 to 500. The same seam stands at the same metre; it is worth looking at now.
- **The ceiling it is tuned against went from 15% of what the work earns to 200%.** On a real day that was 522 credits against the ledger's own 4,310, and no table under that line was worth selling. What the ceiling protects is untouched: every term is weighted tokens times a rate, so a mine nobody works still pays nothing. Re-enumerated as exact rationals over all 501 steps, the worst is 191.750%.
- **The gems have their own ladder back.** They were squashed to 1 / 2 / 3 because quartz cannot go under one whole credit and a cell could only pay 2.75. At 36.72 a cell they are 1 / 3 / 7 again, so a rarer gem is a different event rather than a bigger number.
- **The ladder is 500 steps where it was 100.** Both ends are pinned by the screen and not by taste: ten swings a stone at the bottom, six ordinary replies of lookahead at the top. So a longer ladder is a finer one, not a faster one. 5 credits at the first rung and 17,072 at the last.
- **Every pickaxe in the field keeps its rung, its keys and its depth.** Step 70 of a hundred is step 350 of five hundred. The record carries the length of the ladder its step was counted on, so the rescale works both ways.
- **An unreloaded 1.41.0 window cannot take your pickaxe away.** VS Code updates an extension globally while an open window keeps running the old code, and that build drops any step past its own hundred to zero before writing the record back. The record now carries the tier beside the step, which is the field that build reads, so the worst a stale window can do is put you at the first step of the band you were in.
- **The four pick drawings are spread over the climb** instead of crowding its first seventh: steps 1, 50, 175 and 350 where 10, 35 and 70 were.
- **The Coin Market has a door of its own, and the games list is four.** The list answers "what shall I play", and that answer is a stake laid down and a round that ends. A position is not a round: it is bought and then it is yours, and it moves while the panel is shut. Nothing about the screen changed. If you had starred it, the star is kept.
- **The crossroads stands two doors to a row.** Four across is 62px a column at the design width and 37px at a narrow sidebar, where the shop's drawing paints at four hundredths of its size and the word Market does not fit its column at all. Two to a row gives every door the same height on the same floor, and the drawings are the largest they have been since there were two.
- **Escape leaves the market now.** While a game is open the page hands the key to that game, and nothing on the coin list was listening.
- 14 suites and 55 webview modes pass. Eleven mutations were run against them and all were caught, including both halves of the raise frozen as literals so neither can move without the other.

## 1.40.0: The mine is a mine now, and the pickaxe is in the crab's hands

- **1.39.0 shipped the shaft as two flat rectangles and that was wrong.** A black box for the ground already dug, a grey box for the ground ahead, a line between them, and nothing else: no ceiling, no floor, nothing for the crab to stand on. The band is rebuilt out of the mine entrance picture that already hangs on its own door, so the screen and the door are the same drawing. Broken rock in two courses, a mirrored pair so the repeat does not read as brickwork, timber frames of a cap beam and two posts standing every 32px with bare rock ceiling between them, a dirt floor with sleepers laid at uneven intervals, and a jagged face rather than a straight edge. Not one pixel of it was drawn for this release; all four tiles are cut from that one picture.
- **The pickaxe is held and swung.** It was possible to spend twelve thousand credits and have the screen look exactly as it did before, because the only thing the engine told the screen was whether a pickaxe existed at all. It now says which one, and the tool rides on the crab's claw through all sixteen frames of the swing, pinned at one point so the haft cannot leave the body. The sprite's own arc stays as the trail the tool leaves. Each tier is a different tool rather than the same tool in a different colour: one blade becomes two, then the haft gains a band, then the whole thing turns gold and catches the light. Bare hands remove the tool and the mark, and the crab keeps swinging, because it really is still digging at an eighth of the speed.
- **The strike says which pickaxe struck.** A mark breaks against the face on the two frames where the arc is widest, sized and coloured by tier, carrying an ink halo so it survives on the brightest rock in the wall.
- **A tier is a key, not a timer.** All four picks dig at one speed, so the only thing a better one used to buy was a later date for the bare hands: a postponement rather than a reward. A tier now decides what the wall gives up. Gems come out for anyone including bare hands, fevers from the second rung, coins from the third, relics only from the fourth. Seams out of reach are drawn with the rock still over them, so what a better pick would open is visible before it is bought. A pick worn through opens nothing, since it is a tool and not a receipt.
- **The ceiling is re-proven per tier, and the prices moved because of it.** The same exact enumeration now runs five times: bare hands and each rung come out at 3.63%, 3.63%, 4.08%, 7.24% and 12.92% of what the same tokens earn in the ledger, all under the engine's own 15%. Gating relics away from the lower rungs took their lifetime earnings down with them, so the first pick could no longer pay for itself at 100. Raising what gems pay would have eaten the whole margin under the ceiling, so the prices came down to what each rung can actually pull out instead: 40, 200, 1,500 and 12,500, returning 1.19, 1.35, 1.77 and 2.18 times their cost. The seam table itself is untouched.
- **A pickaxe no longer throws away what is left of the last one.** Fitting a new one used to discard the old one's unspent durability, which made the best moment to buy the moment the game felt worst. The remainder carries over, measured against the old anchor so that a window holding a stale ledger reaches exactly as far as one holding a fresh one. Records written before this release are repaired on load rather than trusted, or every pickaxe in the field would break the instant it was upgraded.
- **The crab holds its own drawing, the engine holds the numbers.** The screen is handed the tier, the ladder's height, the pickaxe's name, which seams are locked and what the offer would open, and it works none of that out for itself.
- The mine's door carries a BETA mark, and a door can carry one because a door is data: no shared file learned the name of a game to put it there.
- The rock tiles are doubled so they resolve on a retina display, which is how the crab was already drawn. At 1:1 the browser stretched them and the wall came out soft beside a sprite that was sharp.
- A fever marker sat at 1.15:1 against the brightest rock, which is invisible. It gets the ink socket the other markers have, and reads at 4.23:1.

## 1.39.0: A mine the tokens you already spent are digging, and a crab that says how hard you are working

- **There is a third door at the crossroads.** Behind it a shaft runs to the right, and what drives it forward is the work already done: the tokens this machine has spent. A pickaxe is bought with credits and wears out as the shaft advances; gems and relics in the ground pay credits back; bare hands dig an eighth as fast; and a fever buries a stretch of ground in one go. Nothing is wagered and nothing is lost, which is why it is not in with the card games.
- **Nothing in it is counted, all of it is derived.** The depth, what is left of the pickaxe, whether the hands are bare and what is lying ahead are all functions of the ledger's own weighted token total, so two windows agree without talking and there is no per-window counter to pay out of twice. The record holds only what cannot be undone: the pickaxe and the token count it was fitted at, how far the claims have reached, what the fevers granted, and the two halves of the money. A claimed stretch is a boundary and not a list, because depth only rises and a list is the one thing two windows must never merge.
- **What it can pay is bounded and the bound is proven rather than asserted.** Enumerated exactly over the gem table, the mine adds at most 12.9% to what the same tokens earn in the ledger, under a ceiling of 15% read off the engine's own constant. A stretch of ground pays once. A month left unopened does not hash a hundred thousand cells on the next keystroke.
- **The crab now reports how hard the machine is working, which is what it was always meant to report.** Nothing running is quiet, something running is typing, a lot of it is working, harder than that is burning, and genuinely overworked is spent. One ladder, five rungs, read off the last hour of activity with a ten minute window. Over seven days of this owner's own transcripts that comes out 41.9%, 29.4%, 18.5% and 10.2% of the time spent working, in that order. What it replaced ran the hardest state 22.3% of the time and the ordinary one 2.8%.
- **The two kinds of account stopped mattering.** What is measured is the work, not a limit, so an account with a five hour window and one without read the same ladder. The strain function, the pace mapping and the two column table are gone, and the test now reads the source to check that the crab's five functions mention no limit at all. Every bar, ring, badge and banner that reports a limit is exactly where it was.
- Quiet is five minutes rather than three. Its three was set at 2.7 times the p99 gap between replies, and measured again on 12,169 gaps that p99 is 133 seconds, not 67.5. It also matters more than it did: with one ladder, three minutes of rest inside a hard run falls all the way to quiet, where the old table hid it.
- The crossroads boxes are 1.7:1 rather than 2:1. At three doors on a 250px sidebar the old ratio drew each picture at a twentieth of its size, which is a smudge rather than a drawing; the new one is a tenth and the row is 16px shorter than it was with two.

## 1.38.2: The crab was flat on its back a fifth of the time

- **An account with no five-hour window is measured on the pace its week is being spent at, and that pace climbed onto the top step far too early.** One multiplication, pace times fifty, put despair at 1.4 times an even pace and the collapse at 1.8, and ran into the ceiling at 2.0. Replayed over 1,105 of this reader's own stored samples, six days of them: flat on its back 22.3% of the time, standing at the working step 2.8%. The hardest state was the commonest, the ordinary one was invisible, and a weekly bar reading 19% sat under a crab that had given up. An account with a five-hour window never had this: it is measured on that window's own percentage, where 90 means ninety percent and nothing was wrong.
- **The climb above an even pace is stretched, and the thresholds did not move.** They could not: the same 50, 70 and 90 are what a five-hour window is read against, where they are correct. What changed is only how a pace is laid onto that scale. Despair is 1.75 times an even pace now and the collapse is 2.5, which over the same six days comes out idle 53.6%, working 22.8%, despair 23.6% and collapsed none at all.
- **None at all is the answer, not a gap.** The hardest pace those six days reached was 2.10 times even. What still lays that account's crab out is a week that is genuinely spent, which is the one case the top step was named for and the one the shelf calls `Overloaded`. Stretching further was measured too and drains the step below it: at 3.0 times, despair falls to 14.8%, and at 3.5 it empties.

## 1.38.0: The shelf says how hard you are driving it, not how much is left

- **`Limit used up` was printed under a weekly bar reading 19%.** The four lines under the scene names were rewritten in 1.37.0 to say how much of the limit was gone, and the limit is not what picks a drawing. There is one set of thresholds and two different quantities go through it: an account with a five-hour window is measured on that window's own percentage, and an account with no window is measured on the pace its week is being spent at, where the middle of the scale is spending the week exactly evenly and the top of it is 1.8 times that. Both of those are real readings and neither is the other, so they can only share a scale in order to pick one set of drawings from one set of thresholds. Any line naming a percentage of the limit is therefore false for one of the two accounts, whatever the wording: the second kind can stand on the top step having spent a fifth of its week.
- **So the four say what is true on both, which is how hard the account is being driven.** `Light load`, `Steady load`, `Heavy load`, `Overloaded`, with `Nothing answered` above them unchanged. The names are unchanged too: the scenes are Quiet, Typing, Working, Burning and Spent, because what a name points at is the crab, and the crab is what it was. The bar on the panel still says how much of the limit is gone; the shelf says what raises each picture, and the two were never answering one question.
- **What is still a reading rather than a wording.** `Overloaded` is true for an account spending its week at nearly twice an even pace, and that is also true a few hours into a week in which very little has been spent at all, because a pace needs a denominator and the first hours of a week barely have one. `Limit used up` was obviously false there and got reported within a day; `Overloaded` is not false there, so the words will not report it again. Whether a crab flat on its back is the right thing to show somebody who rested for a week and then did an hour's work is a question about the thresholds and the floor under that denominator, and nothing about either moved in this version.
- **The drawing that turns up now and then was bought, switched on, and never once seen.** Three things were taking it, and raising how often it comes would have fixed the smallest of them. It is in the Usage tab and nowhere else, and the tick that shows it only looked at whether the window was visible, so a showing that started while the games or the agents tab was open spent its four seconds, and the four minutes of silence owed after it, in front of nobody. The same went for a crab hidden from the settings sheet. A showing now starts only while the crab is actually on screen, and the count towards the next one pauses with it rather than running down unseen. One already running is left alone: pressing a tab does not snatch it away mid-showing.
- **And it was shut out of both of the steps it most often sits in.** It never covered despair or stunned, because there the drawing is the message. That holds for the top step, which is where a genuinely spent week arrives, and it does not hold for the one below: that step is reached at a load of 70, which for an account with no five-hour window is 1.4 times an even pace and can be true at a fifth of the week. There was no warning there to protect, so that step is open now and the top one is not. The burn-rate bubble still blocks one, for a reason that is geometry rather than judgement: its tail is aimed at the top of the ink of whatever is underneath it.
- **The market took the keyboard once a second from a reader who had never touched it.** That screen repaints on its own beat, and each repaint asked whether its own repaint had dropped the focus by reading `document.activeElement === document.body`. The body is also the answer when the focus has simply never been in that panel, so a sidebar that had been clicked anywhere at all had a control in the market focused every second, and because a focus scrolls its element into view, the page moved under the pointer while somebody was reaching for something on it. 1.36.1 closed the half of this where the whole webview did not hold the focus and the keyboard was being pulled out of the chat input; this is the other half, inside a webview that does hold it. The question is now asked of the focus's own history, the element it last landed on and the ancestors it had at the time, so a panel the focus has never been in is never handed it back. And a focus handed back no longer scrolls: the page stays where the reader left it.
- Past that, it is twice as often: four minutes between two of them rather than five, and a flat one in four hundred seconds after that rather than one in nine hundred. Mean gap 10.7 minutes of a panel somebody could see, median 8.6, nine in ten under 20. The floor may not go below the three minutes that decides whether a crab is working or quiet, so that one stretch of quiet never holds two of them, and four minutes is the smallest round number that keeps it.

## 1.36.0: Prices come off a ladder, and the rung is how much was drawn

- **The two drawings that arrived yesterday were free, and they should not have been.** The sleeping crab is 1,000 credits and the black hole is 10,000. Nothing that was already free has a price put on it: every scene still falls back to a drawing that costs nothing, so a panel with nothing bought looks exactly as it did.
- **There are four prices and there will not be a fifth without a reason.** 1K, 10K, 100K, 1000K, and which rung a drawing stands on is its frame count: four frames, eight, sixteen, thirty two. That is the one thing about a drawing that is not an opinion, so nobody has to argue about how good one is, and a drawing redrawn with more frames in it fails a test rather than shipping at a price that no longer matches what was drawn. The top two rungs are empty today.
- **Ten times apart rather than twice, because a price here is a two or three character tag.** `1K` beside `10K` reads as a different kind of thing; doubling reads as noise. Frames double while the price goes up tenfold, since drawing twice as many frames is far more than twice the work.
- **The random row can carry a price now, and the black hole does.** Before it is bought that row is empty and nothing turns up, which is the same bargain every scene makes: the drawing you bought is the drawing you get. ## 1.34.0: Sell part of a coin, and a week that is not seven of the same day

- **A buy chose its size and a sale did not.** Selling a coin sold the whole position, every lot of it, and the only way to take some profit off the table was to take all of it. - **The days are not the same size any more.** Each coin's day gets a multiplier on how far it moves, so a week holds a day at 0.43x of that coin's usual size and a day at 1.75x of it: on the wildest coin, a 173 basis point day and a 700 basis point day in the same week. - **Sharing asked for one thing and is now being asked for two.** The question put to you when sharing was offered names one purpose, that the other PCs signed in to your own Claude account can see your weekly figures, and somebody who agreed to that agreed to that. An account that was already sharing is asked too, because it has answered the first question and not this one. The answer travels with each record rather than being read at the time of the count, so turning it off does not leave your earlier figures in the aggregate and turning it on does not pull in the ones from before you did.
- **What that aggregate is for, since it is a fair thing to ask.** Every figure this extension gives about a shared account rests on how much of the weekly limit a dollar of use costs, and that number is borrowed from the API price list, which the limit does not follow: read cleanly on three accounts it came out 0.0319, 0.0364 and 0.0462 percent per dollar, a 1.45x spread, which is why those figures are bounds rather than readings. Fitting a weight per token kind across many accounts replaces the borrowed number with a measured one, and the same fit is what would show that Anthropic had changed it. ## 1.33.0: The raw token counts, kept apart from the price list

- **Everything this extension says about a shared account's weekly limit goes through one number, how much of the limit a dollar of use costs, and that number is borrowed rather than measured.** The dollars are API list prices; the weekly limit is not spent in proportion to them. ## 1.32.6: A reading every time something moved, instead of one every ten minutes

- **The two bounds behind a shared account's figures are read off the gaps between readings, and the gaps were ten minutes wide.** One of them wants a gap this PC sat out whole; the other wants a gap nobody else was in. Ten minutes is long enough for both to have happened inside one, and a gap like that says nothing and is thrown away. The percentage is polled every sixty seconds anyway, and a reading is now kept whenever anything moved, so roughly a tenth as many gaps have something in them from both sides.
- **A quiet stretch is still one row.** Where neither the account's percentage nor this PC's spend has moved, the last reading's clock is pushed forward instead of a row being added: the gap it closes is described exactly as before, and an hour of an idle machine costs one row rather than sixty. The series grows with activity rather than with time.
- The cap went from 800 readings to 10,000. 800 was sized for one every ten minutes and is thirteen hours at sixty seconds, which would have thrown away most of a week.

## 1.32.5: Turning sharing on no longer changes what this PC did

- **A lone sharing PC read one number with sharing off and another with it on.** Sharing adds PCs to a table; it does not change what this PC spent, so the two have to agree. The sheet with sharing off already showed the tighter of two bounds, and the table ignored it and worked from one of them. The row is now that same figure, whichever way the switch is set.
- **Two bounds, tight in opposite conditions, and the smaller one wins.** The week minus what was witnessed (the account's percentage moving while this PC spent nothing) is tight on an account where this PC is rarely alone, because idle stretches are then plentiful. This PC's spend times the lowest percent-per-dollar any interval showed is tight where it often is alone, because an interval close to alone then exists. An account of five people and an account of one are opposite cases, and taking the smaller covers both.
- **The lowest ratio is the one nearest the truth, and that is arithmetic rather than a guess.** Between two readings the account's percentage rose for everyone while only this PC's spend is known here, so percentage-over-spend is never below the real figure and equals it when nobody else was working. Other people can only push it up.
- **It is still an upper bound.** Intervals where this PC and somebody else were both working cannot be divided between them, and nothing at all is recorded while the PC is off. The number is at most this, and it tightens on its own as more PCs on the account start sharing: a table of two or more goes back to the exact division, which needs no bound and no estimate.

## 1.32.4: What one PC saw of a week it did not spend

- **1.32.3 took what somebody else spent off the account before dividing the rest, and on a table of two PCs that was the other PC.** The measurement is "the account's percentage moved while THIS PC spent nothing", which is exactly everybody else when one PC shares and includes a teammate who was simply working while you were not when two do. - **A lone sharing PC reads what it can prove rather than the whole account.** Its row is the week minus the part of it that moved while that PC was spending nothing: 55 points witnessed against an account at 75 leaves 20, not 75. No rate is involved, so nothing differs between screens, and the sheet says the row is an upper bound because nothing is recorded while the PC is off.
- **The percentage one dollar buys is not a constant that could have been used instead.** On three accounts whose own rate can be read cleanly, each account's week over that whole account's spend, it came out 0.0319, 0.0364 and 0.0462 percent per dollar: a spread of 1.45x. - **A table of two or more is unchanged.** Those rows are the account's percentage divided by what each PC spent, as they have been since 1.30.0, and that is exact when the PCs in the table are everyone. ## 1.32.3: A sharing PC was given the whole account to carry

- **One PC sharing, other people on the account not running this, and the row with that PC's name on it read the entire account's week.** 1.4B tokens and $474 of API-equivalent use drawn as 75% of an Ultra week, where the same work on an account whose PCs all share measures about 15%. - **The divisor is the table's spend where it should be everyone's.** Everyone's cannot be read from here, and it is no longer guessed at either. What CAN be read is already on the PC: between two readings of the account's weekly percentage, the percentage rose by some amount and this PC's own spend rose by some amount, and an interval where the second is zero and the first is not is an interval this PC sat out while the week moved anyway. An account whose PCs all share sees a floor of zero and a screen identical to before.

## 1.31.0: A market that moves while you are looking at it

- **The price moved once a minute and the chart redrew once a second, which is the wrong way round.** On a one-hour candle chart, a minute of the clock slid all 24 bodies one place along while the newest close, the only figure that is news, moved once an hour. The picture was busy and the number was still.
- **The lattice's step is now a 64th of a minute instead of a minute.** The printed price of a coin changes on 94% to 96% of the seconds it exists for, measured over 20,000 consecutive seconds a coin, against 1.7% before.
- **Not one stored price moved.** 64 is a power of two, so the new grid is an exact subdivision of the old one: every minute boundary is still a block edge, the per-step variance falls by exactly 64 while the lattice grows by exactly 6 levels, and the two cancel to the last bit. 160,712 prices were compared against a table taken off 1.30.1 before the engine was touched, and none of them differ. That is the whole reason it is a 64th and not a second: a second is not a power of two, the minute boundary stops being a block edge, and every `entryTicks` already in storage would price a position at a ratio nobody bought at.
- **A single step of the price still cannot clear the fee.** The widest of 200,000 consecutive steps a coin is 0.0243% on Tidether and 0.0601% on Crabidia against a round trip of 0.5%, so the share of steps whose own move pays for a trade is 0.000% on all four coins. A finer grid is more chances at a smaller number, and both figures are asserted rather than reasoned about.
- **The candles sit on a grid of their own step and only the newest one is part way through.** It spans the grid step to now, so it is short, and its close is the price the extension will trade at. The ones behind it no longer move at all, which also fixes the change figure: the base it is read against moves once a step instead of once a minute, so the percentage stopped drifting while the price stood still.
- **One-minute candles have wicks now.** A minute is 64 steps, so it is sampled like any other candle; the high and low of a one-minute candle used to be its open and its close.
- **The panel asks the host for prices every second instead of once a minute.** One reply costs 0.21ms to 0.31ms depending on the window, which is 13ms to 19ms of a minute's CPU. A refusal and the verdict of a trade now stay on screen until something else is pressed, because a message cleared by the next quiet redraw is a message nobody can read.
- **Prices at the coin's own depth are printed to the tick.** `299.49` rather than `287`: a tick is a hundredth of a credit, and rounding to whole credits threw away the digits that move. Rounded, the printed price changes on 5.3% to 8.4% of seconds; to the tick, on 94.3% to 96.2%. The list of coins keeps whole credits, because two decimals there would push a coin's name out of its row.
- **The amount box opens on a tenth of your purse, cut to two figures.** It opened on 100 for every purse: the other games' minimum bet, left behind when 1.24.0 made a credit worth ten times as much, and 0.024% of a balance that has been played for a while. On 410,862 credits the box now offers 41,000.
- **The unit buttons are inside the amount box, and the second one is the coin's own code.** `41000` then `cr | CRAB`, so what the number is is answered where the number is. They were a separate rounded box beside the amount, which at a narrow sidebar stacked into three identical boxes down the screen with the only explanation 44px below. The row is 70px instead of 100px, and the panel 530px instead of 557px.
- **How much of the coin you hold is a line of its own.** `347.9 CRAB` in full-strength type under the profit, rather than the first clause of a muted line that ran to two lines at a narrow sidebar. What stays on that line is the stake, the price it went in at and the age.
- **The coins have new names.** Tidether, Kelpereum, Shelliba and Crabidia, which say what they are jokes about while staying in the sea the crab lives in. The four-letter codes are unchanged, and they could not change: a code is the seed of a coin's price history and the key its position is stored under.
- Volatility is untouched at 1.5%, 2.4%, 3.2% and 4%. The usable ceiling is 412 basis points, twelve above the roughest coin, and the chart scales to the window's own low and high, so a louder coin draws the same picture: the median candle body is 10.1% of the chart on the calmest coin and 8.9% on the wildest.

## 1.29.0: The second account's own folder

- **The forge ladder was far too long.** Reaching +10 used to be years of play. It is now about ten weeks at a typical day's earnings, the fee curve is gentler at the bottom and steeper at the top, and a failed strike heats the forge: six bars, each worth five points on the next attempt, cleared when you succeed. The danger does not move. Heat buys its odds out of the drops and the stays, never out of the break.
- **A success and a break now say what they cost.** The sword's price jumps on the chip at the moment it goes up, and a break prints everything that sword had eaten. The list row carries the level of the sword you are holding.
- **Dice**: **Roll Under** (drag a line anywhere from 1 to 96 and the roll lands on the same ruler you set it with; the side that wins is the side that is filled in), **Sic Bo** (three dice, one bet: small, big, any triple, or an exact total, and the cell the dice actually landed in lights up so a loss says why), **Hazard** (roll as often as you dare, each pip drops into the tray, and a 1 empties it) and **Shut the Box** (nine tiles, two dice, close any set that adds up to the roll; the tiles you cannot use this throw cannot be pressed, so there is nothing to add up).
- **Machines**: **Slots** (three real rings of 24, rolled to the position the extension picked), **Plinko** (twelve rows, thirteen cups, three risk tables, and the bead speeds up as it falls), **Wheel** (every slice has its multiplier written on it, the wide ones pay nothing and the thin one pays twelve) and **Scratch Card** (a 3x3 card you rub a square at a time, and nothing is claimed until all nine are open).
- **Card tables**: **Red Dog**, where two cards are dealt apart and the bet is whether the third falls between them.
- **Favourites**: star any game and it moves to the top of the list, out of its family. Stars follow you between windows. Each family heading folds, so a list of fourteen can be four rows and four headings.
- **Every game with a pay table returns between 96% and 97%**, the band the first four already kept, and no single rung, cup, segment or stopping rule is better than another. Each table was solved by counting every outcome rather than sampled, and a game whose table drifts out of the band refuses to start instead of paying wrong. The market is the one game without a table, so it has no "one round" to return anything: what it has instead is a price that cannot be beaten by any rule for selling, proved rather than sampled, and the 0.5% round trip on top of that.
- Credits are still earned by using Claude Code and nothing else. They cannot be bought and they cash out to nothing.

## 1.18.0: The sword forge, and art on the list

- **Sword Forge**: the crab is a blacksmith at this table. Pay the fee and the sword goes up a level, or stays where it is, or drops back one, and from the level its blade starts to glow a failed strike can break it and take the whole thing. Ten levels. A broken sword is gone and a fresh one costs nothing, so what you lose is everything you fed the old one.

## 1.17.0: Draw Poker joins the table

- **Draw Poker**: a third game at the crab's table. Jacks or better: five cards come up face up, you mark the ones to keep and one draw replaces the rest. Keeping all five is allowed, a pair of tens or less is nothing, and a paid pair of jacks hands your bet straight back.
- **The pay table is on the felt**: all nine rows sit above the cards, and the row you were paid for lights up once the last card is face up. It is an 8/5 table, so the royal pays 800 and the house keeps about 3%, the same band as the other two games. Every multiplier is a whole number, so nothing in the payout is rounded.
- **Marking cards costs nothing**: click a card or press 1 to 5. The marks never leave the panel and travel with the draw as one move, so there is no round trip per card. Enter deals and draws.
- **Games list**: three rows now. Blackjack and High-Low are unchanged.

## 1.16.0: High-Low joins the table

- **High-Low**: a second game at the crab's table. One card lies face up and you call whether the next one beats it. Aces are high, a tie carries the pot on to the new card, and you can cash out after any call. A wrong call costs your opening bet and nothing else, because the pot it grew was the house's money all along.
- **Prices that come from the shoe**: each side is priced off the cards actually left in the six decks, less the house's 3%, so the odds move as the run goes on and no card and no side is ever the better bet. A call that cannot lose returns the pot instead of shrinking it, and what a run leaves is carried into the next one.
- **Games list**: the credits line opens a list with both games on it. Blackjack is unchanged.

## 1.15.0: Credits and a crab dealer

On first run, past sessions are counted once in the background, as far back as your transcripts go, so you start with what you have already earned. The balance sits in the header next to a coin that fills toward the next credit; hover for today's earnings. Hide it from the settings gear.

The extension deals and judges; the panel only draws. Credits cannot be bought or cashed out, and a round abandoned by reloading the window is void.
